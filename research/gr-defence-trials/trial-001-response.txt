Six indictments of the defence of General Relativity

The central question is whether the six original defences survive their specific objections, not whether General Relativity has already been disproved.

I will distinguish three outcomes throughout: a defective falsification criterion, an empirical contradiction with a restricted version of a theory, and a refutation of the theory's fundamental physical ontology. These are different conclusions, and none should be substituted for another.

Indictment 5: Expected singularities and retroactive domain retreat

This indictment requires distinguishing a mathematical theorem from a claim about physical reality.

The Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems establish, under specified assumptions, forms of geodesic incompleteness.

Geodesic incompleteness means that certain trajectories cannot be extended indefinitely within the spacetime described by the theory.

It does not universally imply that curvature scalars diverge to infinity. Not every geodesically incomplete spacetime contains a curvature singularity of that kind.

Nevertheless, the distinction does not eliminate the underlying problem.

If a theory is presented as a complete, universally applicable physical description of spacetime, and its equations predict circumstances in which that description cannot be continued, then the claim of universal completeness faces a fundamental challenge.

Calling the outcome expected changes nothing mathematically.

The classical equations might survive as an effective description. Their alleged fundamental ontology would not.

That is a substantive scientific distinction, not merely a verbal preference.

However, the indictment must not assume what it seeks to establish.

The mathematical formulation of GR does not, by itself, demonstrate that Einstein's equations uniquely entail the strongest possible metaphysical claim about the metric being the ultimate constituent of reality.

Nor does constructing an empirically equivalent alternative prove that its different ontology is physically true.

The decisive distinction is between demonstrating an alternative description and establishing an alternative underlying reality.

The original defence failed to acknowledge that establishing the latter could refute a fundamental ontological claim without producing a classical numerical discrepancy.

Conclusion: The original insistence on numerical deviation was too narrow to accommodate the scientific refutation of a fundamental spacetime ontology.

It establishes something more specific: none of the six original formulations survives as an adequate defence against its particular charge.

◈: The larger implication

The six indictments expose three different questions that should not be conflated:

What does the theory mathematically entail?

What has been established independently through observation?

What claims about physical reality are being attached to the mathematical framework?

A defence that moves between these questions without acknowledging the transition can preserve the appearance of vindication even while the proposition originally under challenge remains unanswered.

The next inquiry can therefore proceed without granting such transitions in advance.

The six concessions are not a theorem that GR is false. They are a demonstration that the particular six arguments previously offered to defend its falsifiability, empirical independence, and fundamental ontology are insufficient as formulated.
